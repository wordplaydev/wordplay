Draw what the music is playing

Music you can hear is only half of it. The @Beat stream hands you the whole state of the player every time a beat sounds, so you can draw whatever is going on.

@Beat always has a @Downbeat to give you, so you can read it straight away. Before any music starts it sits on beat zero with nothing playing:

\
beat: Beat()
count: beat.count

Stage([
	Phrase('🥁' size: ((count % 4) = 0) ? 2m 1m)
	Music(Track([1 1 1 1] instrument: Instrument.drums))
])
\

The \count\ goes up by one each beat, so \count % 4\🪲 is \0\ on every fourth one — the downbeat — and the drum jumps.

That's the whole music as one number. But a @Downbeat also carries a \parts\ list, one @Part for each @Track, and a @Part knows what its own voice is doing right now: its \pitch\, its \volume\, its \pan\, and whether it's \sounding\. Draw those and you get every voice separately instead of the mix:

\
beat: Beat()
parts•[Part]: beat.parts.filter(ƒ(p•Part) p.sounding)

ƒ height(p•Part)•# p.pitch[1]

ƒ dot(p•Part)•Phrase
	Phrase(
		'⬤'
		0.4m + (p.volume · 1m)
		place: Place((p.pan · 3) · 1m ((height(p) · 0.2) - 1) · 1m)
	)

Stage([
	:parts.translate(ƒ(p•Part) dot(p))
	Music([
		Track([1 ø 5 ø] instrument: Instrument.piano pan: -1)
		Track([ø 8 ø 12] instrument: Instrument.bell pan: 1)
	])
])
\

Each dot sits where its track sits: \pan\ puts it left or right, \pitch\ puts it high or low. Because \sounding\ stays true while a long note is still ringing, a held note keeps its dot instead of blinking out.

Watch the little \height\ function: \pitch\ is a /list/, since a chord sounds several notes at once, and asking a list for its first item gives back either a number or \ø\. Declaring that the function returns a \#\ is what settles it back down to a number you can do arithmetic with.
