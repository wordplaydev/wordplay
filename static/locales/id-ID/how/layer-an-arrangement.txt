Layer parts into an arrangement

One @Track is a tune. Several played together is a band — and the trick to writing one is that the parts don't have to be different music.

Because degrees are steps of a scale rather than fixed pitches, a whole bass line can be the melody's own numbers dropped an octave. @Track/key shifts a single track by a number of semitones, and twelve semitones is exactly an octave:

\
tune: [1 5 8 5 6 3 1 ø]

Music([
	Track(tune instrument: Instrument.flute)
	Track(tune instrument: Instrument.synthBass key: -12semitones volume: 60%)
])
\

Two voices, one list. Edit the tune and the bass follows it, because there's only one tune to edit.

Now fill in around it. Chords want their own rhythm rather than the melody's, and a track's own @Track/beat gives it one; \pan\🪲 spreads the voices left and right so the ear can pick them apart:

\
tune: [1 5 8 5 6 3 1 ø]

Music([
	Track(tune instrument: Instrument.flute pan: 0)
	Track(tune instrument: Instrument.synthBass key: -12semitones volume: 60%)
	Track([{1 3 5} ø {1 4 6} ø] instrument: Instrument.synthPad beat: 2beats volume: 35%)
	Track([1 ø 2 ø 1 ø 2 3] instrument: Instrument.drums beat: 0.5beats volume: 45%)
])
\

Watch the volumes: only the flute is at full. Parts at full volume don't add up to a louder band, they add up to a muddier one, because everything sounding at once has to fit through the same speaker. Start the melody at \100%\ and tuck everything else under it.

The pad's four entries at \beat: 2beats\🪲 fill eight beats, and the drums' eight at \0.5beats\ fill four — so the drums come around twice per pad cycle and everything meets again at the top. When parts drift out of step, it's usually because their lengths don't divide into each other.
